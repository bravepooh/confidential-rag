# Cross-method map: four languages for the same moments

Original synthesis written for this project. Not a summary of any single work.

The therapist works across EFT/EFCT, IFS, PACT and AEDP. These models were built
by different people for different purposes, but in a couples session they
frequently describe the *same observable moment* in different vocabulary. This
note maps the overlaps so that guidance can name a moment in whichever language
is most useful, and so retrieval can find related material across modalities.

Treat every mapping below as an approximation. The models genuinely disagree in
places, and those disagreements are noted rather than smoothed over.

## The central overlap: threat shuts down contact

All four models rest on the same observation. When a partner perceives danger in
the relationship, something protective activates, and that protection is what
the other partner actually experiences, not the vulnerability underneath it.

| What you observe | EFT/EFCT | IFS | PACT | AEDP |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Partner escalates, criticises, demands | Pursue move in the cycle | A protector, often a manager or firefighter | Activated arousal, fight-side | Defence against core affect |
| Partner goes quiet, flat, leaves | Withdraw move in the cycle | A protector, often a manager | Activated arousal, flight or freeze-side | Defence, often with inhibitory affect |
| The repeating pattern itself | The negative cycle, the dance | Parts of each partner triggering each other | A two-person system out of secure functioning | An intersubjective field organised by defence |
| The soft feeling under the protection | Primary attachment emotion | An exile | The younger, unregulated state | Core affective experience |
| The moment it finally lands | Softening, bonding event | Unblending and Self-to-Self contact | Repair inside the couple bubble | Transformational affect, undoing aloneness |

## Where the models actually differ

These are not cosmetic differences and the guidance should not paper over them.

**Unit of attention.** EFT and PACT look primarily at the *system between* the
partners: the cycle, the two-person psychological system. IFS looks primarily
*inside* each partner: which part is present, and is Self available. AEDP looks
at the *moment of affective experience* and what happens if it is stayed with.
A session can be read through any of these lenses and they will highlight
different things.

**What drives change.** EFT holds that new bonding interactions, enacted in
session, restructure the attachment bond. Greenberg's emotion-focused lineage
puts more weight on individual emotion processing and self-soothing than
Johnson's attachment-centred version does; this is a real split inside the "EFT"
label, not a branding quirk. PACT holds that change follows from establishing
secure-functioning agreements and regulating arousal in real time. AEDP holds
that change follows from experiencing previously unbearable affect in the
presence of an attuned other, and then *processing the experience of the change
itself*, which is the metatherapeutic move the others do not emphasise.

**Pace and arousal.** PACT deliberately works fast and up close, provoking and
then regulating activation in session. AEDP slows down and stays with a moment.
EFT sits between. IFS asks permission of parts before going anywhere. A
guidance note that recommends an AEDP-style slow deepening in a moment where
PACT would say "the couple is flooded, regulate first" is giving contradictory
advice, so arousal level should be read before pace is recommended.

**Where the therapist stands.** PACT is directive and active. AEDP is explicitly
affirming and self-disclosing about the therapist's own experience. EFT is
process-directive but content-following. IFS asks the therapist to work from
their own Self and to keep their own parts out of the way.

## Reading a couples session: an order of operations

A practical sequence that respects the differences above.

1. **Arousal first.** Is either partner flooded, shut down, or dysregulated? If
   yes, nothing interpretive will land. This is the PACT question and it gates
   everything else.
2. **The cycle.** What is the repeating move-and-countermove? Who pursues, who
   withdraws, and what does each one's move do to the other? This is the EFT
   question, and it is the most reliably useful frame in a first pass.
3. **Who is present.** In each partner, which protective part is speaking, and
   is there any Self-energy available (curiosity, calm, compassion)? This is the
   IFS question and it is especially useful when one partner seems to contain
   several conflicting positions at once.
4. **What almost happened.** Was there a moment of real feeling that appeared
   and then got covered? This is the AEDP question, and it points at where the
   next session could go.
5. **What moved.** Did anything shift, however small: a softer tone, a turn
   toward, a moment of being received? Name it, because naming it is itself an
   intervention in AEDP terms and a consolidation in EFT terms.

## Vocabulary quick reference

Terms the guidance may use, with the model each belongs to, so citations stay
honest about their origin:

- **Negative cycle, demon dialogue, raw spot, attachment injury, softening,
  withdrawer re-engagement, pursuer softening, the EFT tango, stages 1 to 3 and
  the nine steps** belong to EFT/EFCT.
- **Parts, managers, firefighters, exiles, Self and Self-energy, blending and
  unblending, burdens, unburdening** belong to IFS.
- **Secure functioning, the couple bubble, islands, waves and anchors,
  co-regulation, thriving versus surviving, launchings and landings** belong to
  PACT.
- **Transformance, core affect, the triangle of experience, the four states,
  metatherapeutic processing, undoing aloneness, healing affects, true other**
  belong to AEDP.

Using a term outside its home model is fine in clinical thinking, but a citation
should point at the model the term actually comes from.

## Cautions for automated guidance

- Attachment-style labels (island, wave, anchor; anxious, avoidant) are
  heuristics, not diagnoses, and they are routinely misapplied from a single
  session. Offer them as questions, never as classifications.
- The evidence base is uneven. EFCT has the strongest outcome research of the
  four, and even that is weaker under strict criteria than institute marketing
  suggests. IFS, PACT and AEDP have far less controlled outcome evidence. Do not
  present any of these models' claims as settled fact.
- A single recorded session is a thin slice. Patterns named from one hour are
  hypotheses for the therapist to test, not findings.
- Escalation, contempt, or any indication of coercion or violence changes the
  clinical picture entirely and is outside what pattern-matching should comment
  on. Flag it and defer to the therapist.
